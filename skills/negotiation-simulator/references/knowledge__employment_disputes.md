# Employment Disputes

## Common dispute types

- Wrongful termination or unfair dismissal.
- Unpaid wages, overtime, or bonuses.
- Severance negotiation.
- Hostile work environment or harassment.
- Discrimination (age, gender, race, disability, religion).
- Retaliation for whistleblowing or complaints.
- Non-compete or non-disclosure enforcement.
- Misclassification (employee vs. contractor).
- Benefits denial (health insurance, PTO, retirement).
- Unsafe working conditions.

## Core question

Was the employer's action lawful under the applicable employment law, and is there documentation to support the claim?

## General principles (jurisdiction-dependent)

### At-will employment (US)

Most US states are "at-will," meaning either side can end the relationship for any legal reason. But termination is still illegal if it violates:

- Anti-discrimination laws (Title VII, ADA, ADEA).
- Retaliation protections.
- Employment contracts or collective bargaining agreements.
- Public policy (firing someone for reporting safety violations, for example).

### Employment contracts (EU, UK, many countries)

- Employer must follow notice periods and termination procedures.
- Unfair dismissal claims available in many jurisdictions after a qualifying period.
- Redundancy procedures must be genuine.

### China (劳动法 / 劳动合同法)

- Written labor contract required within 30 days of employment.
- No contract = double wages for up to 11 months.
- Employer must pay severance (N or N+1 months) for most terminations.
- Overtime must be compensated: 150% weekday, 200% weekend, 300% holiday.
- Labor arbitration (劳动仲裁) is mandatory before court.

## Evidence checklist

- Employment contract or offer letter.
- Pay stubs and bank records.
- Written communications (emails, messages, memos).
- Performance reviews.
- Termination letter or notice.
- Witness statements.
- Company handbook or policy documents.
- Medical records (if health-related).
- Timeline of events.

## Escalation ladder

1. Internal HR complaint (documented in writing).
2. Written grievance to management.
3. Mediation (internal or external).
4. Government agency complaint (EEOC in US, ACAS in UK, 劳动仲裁 in China).
5. Legal consultation.
6. Formal claim or lawsuit.

## Strong angles

### Procedural failure

The employer did not follow their own handbook, policy, or contractual procedures.

### Documentation gap

If the employer claims performance issues but has no written warnings or reviews showing problems, the termination looks pretextual.

### Retaliation timeline

If adverse action happened shortly after a protected activity (complaint, whistleblowing, leave request), the timing itself is evidence.

### Unpaid compensation

Wage theft is straightforward when documented. Pay stubs, time records, and contract terms speak clearly.

## Saul angle

Employment disputes are emotional, but the strongest cases are built on paper. Before you do anything, gather every email, message, contract, review, and pay stub. The employer's HR team will have their file ready — you need yours to be better. When negotiating severance, remember: they want you to sign a release. That release has value. Do not sign it without getting something in return.
