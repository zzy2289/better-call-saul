# Chargeback and Payment Disputes

## What is a chargeback

A chargeback is a forced reversal of a payment card transaction initiated by the cardholder through their bank or card issuer. It bypasses the merchant and goes through the payment network (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, etc.).

## When a chargeback is legitimate

- Goods or services not received.
- Item significantly not as described.
- Unauthorized transaction (fraud).
- Double-charged or wrong amount.
- Merchant refuses to process a valid refund.
- Subscription charged after confirmed cancellation.

## When a chargeback is NOT appropriate

- Buyer's remorse or change of mind (unless merchant policy allows return and merchant refuses).
- User wants to keep goods and also get money back.
- User has not contacted the merchant first.
- Dispute is about subjective quality rather than objective mismatch.

## Chargeback process (general)

1. Contact the merchant first. Document the attempt.
2. If unresolved, contact your bank or card issuer.
3. File the dispute with the reason code that matches your situation.
4. Provide evidence: receipts, screenshots, tracking info, correspondence.
5. The bank contacts the merchant's acquiring bank.
6. The merchant can accept or contest with a representment.
7. The issuer makes a decision. Some networks allow a second arbitration.

## Key deadlines

- Visa: 120 days from transaction date (or expected delivery date for goods not received).
- Mastercard: 120 days, with some exceptions up to 540 days.
- Amex: 120 days.
- PayPal buyer protection: 180 days.
- Check your specific issuer — some banks have shorter windows.

## Reason codes to know

### Visa
- 13.1: Merchandise/services not received.
- 13.3: Not as described or defective.
- 10.4: Fraud — card-absent environment.

### Mastercard
- 4853: Goods or services not as described.
- 4855: Goods or services not provided.
- 4837: No cardholder authorization.

## Evidence checklist

- Order confirmation / receipt.
- Product listing or service description.
- Photos of item received vs. listing.
- Tracking information.
- All correspondence with merchant (emails, chat logs).
- Cancellation confirmation (for subscription disputes).
- Merchant refund policy screenshot.
- Timeline of events.

## Common pitfalls

- Filing before contacting the merchant — weakens your case and may be rejected.
- Wrong reason code — match your situation precisely.
- Missing the deadline — check and act early.
- Providing too little evidence — more documentation is better.
- Using chargeback as a threat — only file when genuinely warranted.
- Repeated chargebacks — card issuers may flag your account.

## Ombudsman and alternative dispute resolution

### EU

- European Consumer Centre (ECC-Net) for cross-border disputes.
- Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) platform: ec.europa.eu/odr.

### UK

- Financial Ombudsman Service (FOS) for bank/payment disputes.
- Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act: credit card purchases over £100 give joint liability with the merchant.
- Chargeback (Visa/MC scheme) available for debit cards and credit cards under £100.

### US

- Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) for bank complaints.
- Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA): dispute rights for credit card billing errors.
- State attorney general consumer protection offices.

### Australia

- Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA).
- Australian Consumer Law: right to a refund for goods not matching description.

## Saul angle

A chargeback is the nuclear option — use it only after the merchant refuses a reasonable resolution. Document everything before you file. The strongest chargeback cases have a clear paper trail: "I asked, they refused, here is the evidence." Banks side with cardholders who look organized and truthful, not angry. Never file a chargeback to keep goods you should return — that is fraud and it will catch up with you.
