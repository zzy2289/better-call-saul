# Insurance Claims

## Common dispute types

- Claim denied with unclear or generic reasons.
- Lowball settlement offer.
- Delayed claim processing.
- Coverage dispute (what is and is not covered).
- Pre-existing condition exclusion (health insurance).
- Depreciation vs. replacement value disagreement.
- Failure to investigate properly.
- Bad faith practices.
- Cancellation or non-renewal disputes.

## Core question

Is the denial or low offer supported by the actual policy language, or is the insurer interpreting terms in their favor without clear basis?

## Evidence checklist

- Insurance policy (full document, not just the summary).
- Claim filing records and correspondence.
- Denial letter with stated reasons.
- Photos/video of damage or loss.
- Repair estimates or medical records.
- Police or incident reports.
- Third-party assessments or appraisals.
- Communication log with insurer (dates, names, what was said).
- State insurance regulations or consumer guides.

## Key principles

### Policy language controls

The specific wording of the policy determines coverage. Marketing materials and agent promises do not override the policy text.

### Ambiguity favors the insured

In most jurisdictions, ambiguous policy language is interpreted in favor of the policyholder (contra proferentem).

### Duty of good faith

Insurers have a legal duty to handle claims in good faith. Unreasonable delays, lowball offers without basis, or denials that ignore evidence may constitute bad faith.

### Appraisal clause

Many property insurance policies include an appraisal clause. If you disagree with the insurer's valuation, you can invoke appraisal — each side hires an appraiser, and if they disagree, an umpire decides.

## Escalation ladder

1. Call the adjuster and ask for the specific policy language supporting the denial.
2. Write a formal appeal letter with evidence and policy references.
3. Request a supervisor review.
4. File a complaint with the state insurance commissioner or ombudsman.
5. Invoke the appraisal clause (for valuation disputes).
6. Consult a public adjuster or insurance attorney.
7. File a bad faith complaint or lawsuit.

## Strong angles

### Denial without policy citation

```text
Your denial letter references [reason] but does not cite the specific policy provision that excludes this claim. Please provide the exact policy language, section, and page number that supports this denial so I can review it against my copy of the policy.
```

### Lowball offer

```text
The settlement offer of [amount] does not reflect the actual cost of [repair/replacement/treatment]. I have obtained [number] independent estimates showing the cost is [amount]. I am requesting a revised offer that reflects the documented damages. If we cannot agree, I would like to invoke the appraisal clause under Section [X] of the policy.
```

### Unreasonable delay

```text
This claim was filed on [date], now [X] days ago. I have provided all requested documentation. Under [state] regulations, claims must be acknowledged within [X] days and resolved within [X] days. Please provide a status update and expected resolution date in writing.
```

## Saul angle

Insurance companies count on most people giving up after the first denial. The denial letter is not a final answer — it is a negotiating position. Ask for the exact policy language they are relying on. Half the time, they cannot point to a clear exclusion, and that is when your appeal gets traction. Stay calm, stay documented, and remember: the state insurance commissioner exists specifically to handle situations where insurers act unreasonably.
