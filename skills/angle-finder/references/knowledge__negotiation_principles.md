# Negotiation Principles

## Objective first

Before writing any script, identify what the user actually wants.

Common outcomes:

- Refund.
- Replacement.
- Repair.
- Credit.
- Cancellation.
- Lower price.
- Payment.
- Apology.
- Contract change.
- Deadline extension.
- Better service.
- Relationship preservation.

A strong message has one primary ask. Multiple asks weaken the request unless ranked clearly.

## BATNA

BATNA means best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

Ask:

- What happens if the other side says no?
- Can the user switch providers?
- Can the user dispute through a platform?
- Can the user pause work?
- Can the user leave a factual review?
- Can the user use a regulator, ombudsman, or small claims path?

Do not bluff a BATNA the user cannot or will not use.

## ZOPA

ZOPA means zone of possible agreement.

Find a solution both sides can accept:

- Refund instead of replacement.
- Store credit instead of cash.
- Partial refund plus keeping item.
- Payment plan.
- Discount for inconvenience.
- Revised deadline.
- Reduced scope for the same budget.

## Anchoring

When asking for money or concessions, start with a clear, defensible ask.

A defensible anchor ties to:

- Contract price.
- Purchase price.
- Policy promise.
- Comparable market price.
- Documented damage.
- Missed deadline.
- Replacement cost.

Avoid absurd anchors unless the goal is intentionally aggressive and the risk is clear.

## Framing

Frame the request as:

- Reasonable.
- Easy to approve.
- Supported by evidence.
- Less costly than escalation.
- A chance for the other side to fix the issue.

Example frame:

```text
I am not asking for anything beyond the original promise. I am asking for the order to be corrected or refunded based on the documented mismatch.
```

## Evidence beats emotion

Emotion explains urgency, but evidence wins decisions.

Evidence can include:

- Screenshots.
- Receipts.
- Order pages.
- Tracking logs.
- Emails.
- Chat transcripts.
- Photos.
- Contract clauses.
- Dates and timestamps.
- Names or ticket numbers.

## Face-saving exits

Give the other side a way to agree without admitting wrongdoing.

Examples:

- "I understand mistakes happen."
- "This may have been a listing or fulfillment error."
- "I am looking for a practical resolution, not a prolonged dispute."
- "If you can process this as a one-time exception, I am happy to consider the matter closed."

## Escalation ladder

Start with the least costly channel that can solve the problem, then climb.

1. Normal support request.
2. Firm written message with evidence.
3. Supervisor or specialist team.
4. Formal complaint channel.
5. Platform dispute.
6. Payment provider dispute when legitimate.
7. Regulator, ombudsman, consumer protection body, or small claims path when appropriate.
8. Public review or social visibility, factual and measured.

## Deadlines

Deadlines work when they are reasonable and attached to a real next step.

Good:

```text
Please confirm by Friday, June 12. If I do not hear back, I will escalate this through the platform dispute process with the attached evidence.
```

Bad:

```text
Respond in one hour or I will ruin your company.
```

## Written record

When stakes rise, move to writing.

Phone calls can be useful for speed, but written channels create proof.

After a call, send a recap:

```text
Thank you for the call today. To confirm, you stated that the refund was denied because ____. My understanding is that the next step is ____. Please correct me if I misunderstood.
```

## Red lines

Do not:

- Lie.
- Invent damages.
- Threaten illegal action.
- Pretend to have a lawyer.
- Make fake regulatory claims.
- Publicly accuse fraud unless the facts support it.
- Escalate before making a clear reasonable ask.
